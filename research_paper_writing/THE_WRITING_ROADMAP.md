## Part 1 · Before you write — the preparation the courses insist on

Every course in this library makes the same claim in different words: papers are not written
from a blank page, they are *assembled* from reading notes, a research question, and a gap
statement. Wiley's course and the Ref-n-Write playlist both spend their opening videos on
this. Do this preparation and the writing stages of Part 3 become filling-in.

### 1.1 Read papers the way researchers do

The Ref-n-Write course's opening video (1:48) gives the aerial view of the paper — the nine
parts you will later write — and the study-note method below is how this library's
[learning paths](08_LEARNING_PATHS/) tell you to accumulate the raw material.

**The method (three passes, from the library's reading workflow):**

1. **Pass 1 · Triage (5 min/paper)** — title, abstract, headings, and the last paragraph of
   the Introduction (where the research question lives). Decide: does this paper matter?
   Close the "no" papers forever.
2. **Pass 2 · Map (15 min)** — figures, tables, first Discussion paragraph, Methods where
   the design is unclear. Answer: what did they do, what did they find?
3. **Pass 3 · Deep read (45+ min)** — only for pillar papers: the closest competitors and
   your methodological source.

**The reading-note template** (one per paper, in Zotero or a plain file):

```text
PAPER:            [authors, year, title]
THEIR RQ:         [their research question, one sentence]
DESIGN:           [method, sample/data, duration — 2 lines]
KEY FINDING:      [the headline result, WITH the number]
LIMITATION:       [what the authors admit, or what you see as the gap]
QUOTABLE PHRASE:  [one sentence whose PATTERN is worth adapting — never the words]
MY VERDICT:       pillar / supporting / background / discard
```

The **LIMITATION** rows across five to ten notes are, laid side by side, your future gap
statement — that is not a metaphor, it is the mechanism.

**Tools the library recommends for this stage**: [Zotero](06_TOOLS/05_zotero.md) for the
library and notes (free), [SciSpace](06_TOOLS/03_scispace.md) for comprehension while
reading, and the ARES `skills/02_literature_command/citation_network_analysis.md` prompt when
you want the network view of which work is load-bearing.

### 1.2 Find your research question

The Ref-n-Write video #4 (6:20, *How to Formulate Research Questions*) and the library's
[research-questions guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/05_research_questions.md) teach the same
discipline: the RQ is an engineering artifact — **focused, answerable, falsifiable, and sized
to one paper**. Grad Coach's methodology videos (in the
[video-courses folder](02_VIDEO_COURSES/03_grad_coach.md)) treat the coupling between the RQ
and the Methods section explicitly.

**From topic to RQ in three moves** (from the guide):

```text
1. Topic → question:        add what/how/why/when + a measurable object
                            "spaced repetition" → "how does spaced repetition affect exam performance?"
2. Question → focused:      bind to system, population, scale, comparison
                            "...in first-year biology students, vs. massed restudy?"
3. Focused → hypothesis:    state the directional prediction and the test that could
                            refute it (empirical work only)
```

**The four tests every RQ must pass** (from the same guide):

| Test | The question it asks |
|:---|:---|
| **Focused** | One question — or a theme wearing a question's clothes? |
| **Answerable** | Do the data and method exist, or are they collectable by you? |
| **Falsifiable** | Can a result refute it? ("quizzes are good" cannot be refuted) |
| **Sized** | One paper's worth — not a program of research |

**The RQ–Methods test from the guide**: *if your Methods section could be written without
revealing the RQ's answer, the RQ is weak.* Run this test before you commit.

### 1.3 Write the gap statement before any section

The library's [introduction guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/03_introduction.md) names the model:
**Swales' CARS** (Create A Research Space) — *establishing the territory, establishing the
niche, occupying the niche* — from Swales & Feak's
[*Academic Writing for Graduate Students*](03_BOOKS/08_swales_feak_academic_writing_graduate_students.md),
the theory every modern guide (including the Ref-n-Write video #2) uses implicitly. Three
sentences:

```text
TERRITORY:   [Topic] matters because [concrete stake, with citations].
NICHE:       However, it remains unknown whether [the gap — drawn from your
             reading notes' LIMITATION rows].
OCCUPATION:  This study addresses that gap by [method in six words] on
             [population/data], measuring [outcome].
```

These three sentences are the seed of your Introduction (Part 3, §3.3) and the hinge your
whole paper turns on.

### Your work — Part 1

- [ ] Ten reading notes in the template above (verdicts tagged: pillar/supporting/background)
- [ ] One RQ passing all four tests, plus its hypothesis (empirical work)
- [ ] A three-sentence gap statement whose NICHE traces to specific limitation rows

## Part 2 · The academic writing style — the cross-cutting craft

Every manuscript section has its own pattern (Part 3), but the *style* — the sentence-level
English — is one craft, taught before structure by the best course in this library. Stanford's
[*Writing in the Sciences*](01_COURSES/01_stanford_writing_in_the_sciences.md) (4.9★, 9,859
reviews) "starts at the sentence level — clutter, verbs, parallelism — before touching
manuscript structure," and its Units 1–4 are the curriculum below. The books deepen each
lesson; the Phrasebank supplies the phrasings.

### 2.1 Cut the clutter (Stanford Unit 1)

Stanford Unit 1 is "principles of effective writing — cutting clutter, writing clearly and
concisely." The rule it teaches: **delete every word that does no work.** The
[Purdue OWL](04_FREE_GUIDES/01_purdue_owl.md)'s wordiness exercises drill the same cuts, and
this library's [Duke guide](04_FREE_GUIDES/02_duke_graduate_school_scientific_writing.md)
frames the standard memorably: *"If the intent of the writer is communicated, the writing was
effective, regardless of rules kept or broken."*

**The classic first cuts** (the ones every course names):

```text
"in order to"            → to
"due to the fact that"   → because
"it should be noted that" → (delete — just say the thing)
"a total of 12"          → 12
"very", "quite", "really" → (delete)
```

**A note on what cutting means**: clutter is not only extra words — it is words that blur
the actor and the action. "There was a significant increase in the level of expression"
hides both; "Expression doubled" shows them.

### 2.2 Use strong verbs; reunite subject and predicate (Stanford Unit 2)

Stanford Unit 2 teaches "active vs. passive, strong active verbs, reuniting subjects and
predicates." Three applications:

1. **Prefer the active voice where the actor matters.** "We randomized 64 students" beats
   "Randomization was performed on 64 students" — same fact, half the machinery, a visible
   actor. (Passive still has legitimate homes: Methods steps where the actor is obvious, and
   venue norms that require it — match two recent papers from your target journal.)
2. **Turn nominalizations back into verbs.** "made a comparison of" → *compared*;
   "conducted an investigation into" → *investigated*. This is the single highest-yield
   sentence-level edit in the Stanford course's method.
3. **Pick verbs that carry meaning by themselves.** "is, has, performs, achieves" are
   placeholders; specific verbs ("reduces, doubles, outperforms, correlates") do work.

### 2.3 Build the sentence and the paragraph (Stanford Unit 3)

Stanford Unit 3 covers "punctuation (colons, dashes, parentheses, semicolons), parallelism,
focused paragraphs." The two rules this roadmap carries forward:

- **Parallelism**: items in a list keep the same grammatical shape — "we collected, cleaned,
  and analyzed the data" (three verbs), not "we collected data, the data were cleaned, and
  analysis followed." Parallel lists read faster because the reader predicts their shape.
- **One paragraph, one job**: a paragraph is a unit of thought, opened by a sentence that
  states its point (the "topic sentence" the OWL's handouts teach), developed by the
  sentences that follow, and closed before the job changes. The
  [results pattern](07_SECTION_GUIDES/07_results.md) and the
  [synthesis pattern](07_SECTION_GUIDES/04_literature_review.md) in Part 3 are both
  paragraph-level applications of this rule.

### 2.4 Write it as a story (Schimel)

[*Writing Science*](03_BOOKS/02_schimel_writing_science.md) teaches "science as story":
openings, challenge/action/resolution arcs, paragraph-level story flow — the book the
community recommends "for researchers whose papers are technically correct but read flat."
The application in this roadmap: your paper's arc is a question (the gap), a struggle (the
methods), an outcome (the results), and a meaning (the discussion). When a section reads
flat, check whether its *job in the story* is visible in its first sentence.

### 2.5 Learn the moves, then the phrases (Glasman-Deal + the Phrasebank)

[*Science Research Writing*](03_BOOKS/05_glasman_deal_science_research_writing.md) is the
tightest "section anatomy" book in print: "for each [section]: the canonical move structure,
the grammar that performs those moves, and the vocabulary that fills them." The move
structures she teaches are exactly the patterns in Part 3.

Once you know the move you need to perform, the
[Manchester Academic Phrasebank](04_FREE_GUIDES/03_manchester_academic_phrasebank.md) —
"the single highest-utility free URL in this entire library" — supplies the sanctioned
English, organized by rhetorical function. Its six sections map onto the paper:

| Phrasebank section | Use it while writing |
|:---|:---|
| Introducing work | Introduction, abstract opening |
| Describing methods | Methods |
| Reporting results | Results |
| Discussing findings | Discussion |
| Writing conclusions | Conclusion |
| Referring to sources | Literature review, everywhere citations appear |

**How to use it without plagiarizing**: take the *pattern* of a phrase, not the sentence —
swap in your content, your variables, your numbers. The [Ref-n-Write blog](04_FREE_GUIDES/07_refnwrite_blog.md)
(the same team as the anchor playlist) covers exactly this paraphrasing discipline, and MIT's
[21W.794 materials](01_COURSES/09_mit_graduate_technical_writing_workshop.md) set the
source-work standard (paraphrase protocols, not copy-paste).

### 2.6 The ESL edge (Wallwork)

[*English for Writing Research Papers*](03_BOOKS/04_wallwork_english_for_writing_research_papers.md)
is "the world's standard book for the majority of authors who write in English as a second
language," attacking "the specific failure modes of ESL academic prose." Three of its
teachings this roadmap carries:

- **Write short sentences when in doubt** — ambiguity in English grows with sentence length;
  two clear sentences beat one tangled one.
- **Watch the ambiguity traps** — pronouns with two possible antecedents, "only" in the wrong
  position, dangling modifiers ("After analyzing the samples, the bacteria grew…" — the
  bacteria did not analyze anything).
- **Learn what referees criticize** — the book's coverage of "what referees criticize and
  why" maps onto the failure modes named in each Part 3 section.

### 2.7 The order professionals draft (Wallwork + the ARES workflow)

Wallwork's order-of-writing advice — **Methods → Results → … → Abstract (last)** — is the
order this roadmap follows in Part 3, and the reasoning is practical:

1. **Methods first**: you already know it in perfect detail; it is past-tense reporting; it
   builds momentum and exposes design gaps while they are still fixable.
2. **Results second**: the newest facts on paper; the figures get made here.
3. **Then the argument sections**: literature review, discussion, introduction — these need
   the results to argue around.
4. **Conclusion, abstract, title last**: the paper in 3 sentences, then 200, then 15 — each
   a compression of the one before it, so each is written when there is something to compress.

### Your work — Part 2 (the style clinic, one afternoon)

- [ ] Take one paragraph you wrote recently; cut 15% of its words without losing content;
      count before and after
- [ ] Find three nominalizations in your paragraph and restore the verbs
- [ ] Find one passive whose actor matters; make it active
- [ ] Open the Phrasebank's *Describing methods* section; adapt three phrases to your own
      study (pattern, not words)
- [ ] Rewrite one tangled sentence as two short ones (Wallwork's rule)

*(The research question is not repeated here — it is preparation, not a manuscript section;

### 3.1 Title & keywords — *written last, decided early*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #1 · Course Introduction (1:48)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_01_course_introduction.md)
**Read next:** Wiley modules *What Makes a Good Title* (4 lessons) + *Choosing the Right
Keywords* (4 lessons) · [library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/01_title_and_keywords.md)

**What the sources teach.** The title is "the paper's most-read sentence — read in search
results, reference lists, and reviewer triage," and the discovery division of labor is:
"the title persuades humans, keywords persuade machines" (the guide, on why Wiley gives
title and keywords separate modules).

**The six-point title checklist** (from the guide):

1. Names the object of study (system, dataset, organism, theory)
2. Names the method or contribution type (review, meta-analysis, framework, benchmark)
3. Contains the 2–4 terms a searcher would actually query
4. Avoids "Novel", "A Study of", and question marks unless the venue loves them
5. Fits the venue's length norm (many journals cap at ~15 words — check the template)
6. Survives the "alone in a search result" test — needs no surrounding context

**Style rules.** Titles are noun phrases, not sentences; no filler adjectives; every word
earns its place. Keywords do not repeat title words one-for-one — use the discipline's
controlled vocabulary (MeSH, ACM CCS, IEEE Thesaurus) where the venue supports it.

**Your work.** Draft three candidate titles; run the checklist on each; keep the winner.
Draft five keywords that cover the synonyms searchers type but the title omits.

### 3.2 Abstract — *written last*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #9 · How to Write the Abstract Section (5:09)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_09_abstract.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *The Abstract* (5 lessons — the most of any section in their
course) · [library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/02_abstract.md)

**What the sources teach.** The abstract is "a complete mini-paper: background, gap,
methods, key results, and implication, in roughly 150–300 words depending on venue. It is
written *last* and read *first* — most rejection decisions begin here" (the guide).
Nature's summary paragraph cap is 200 words.

**The five-move abstract** (from the guide):

```text
1. CONTEXT      one sentence — the field and why it matters
2. GAP          one sentence — what was unknown or unsolved
3. METHODS      1–2 sentences — approach, data, scale
4. RESULTS      2–3 sentences — actual numbers ("23% faster", never "results are discussed")
5. IMPLICATION  one sentence — what changes now
```

**Style rules that survive every venue** (the guide's list): no citations; no abbreviations
left undefined; numbers over adjectives; it must stand alone (no "as shown in Section 3").

**Watch out for.** The guide's warning is the one editors repeat: the abstract that
discusses results instead of reporting them. Every results sentence should carry a number.

**Your work.** After your Results and Conclusion exist (they must, first), write the
five moves as five physical sentences on five lines, then compress to the venue's cap.

### 3.3 Introduction — *written after the results exist*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #2 · How to Write the Introduction Paragraph (7:50)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_02_introduction.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Introduction* (4 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/03_introduction.md)

**What the sources teach.** The introduction is a funnel: "broad context → specific problem
→ the gap → your contribution → roadmap," on the CARS model (territory, niche, occupation)
from Swales & Feak that "every modern guide including the Ref-n-Write video" uses. And the
stakes: "a reviewer decides within the introduction whether the paper claims something new;
the gap sentence is where that claim lives."

**The funnel, sentence by sentence** (from the guide):

```text
1. TERRITORY   1–3 sentences — the field and its stakes (cite reviews, not textbooks)
2. NICHE       2–4 sentences — what specifically remains unsolved: YOUR GAP STATEMENT
               from Part 1.3 goes here
3. OCCUPATION  3–5 sentences — your question, approach, and finding, plainly, with the
               contribution NAMED
4. ROADMAP     0–1 sentences — "Section 2 reviews…"; some venues consider it filler —
               check recent issues
```

**Style rules.** Present tense for the field ("X is widely used"), past for your study
("we measured"). Territory cites reviews; the niche is where your reading notes'
limitation rows finally speak.

**Watch out for.** The failure the guide names: "introductions that are 80% literature
summary and 20% contribution." Depth belongs in the literature review; the Introduction
points to it.

**Your work.** Paste your Part-1.3 gap statement as the niche block; build the four blocks
around it; check the proportion (territory+others' work ≤ roughly a third of the section).

### 3.4 Literature review — *written early, from the matrix*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #3 · How to Write the Literature Review (7:56)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_03_literature_review.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Introduction* (4 lessons — the review sits inside it) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/04_literature_review.md)

**What the sources teach.** "A literature review is an *argument about the state of
knowledge*, not an annotated list" — and the video's teaching (from its study notes):
organizing prior work **thematically, not chronologically**.

**Choosing the organizing principle** (the guide's list): **thematic** — group by
sub-question or approach (most common in journal articles); **methodological** — group by
technique family, when methods *are* the story; **chronological** — only when the field's
evolution is itself the argument.

**The synthesis paragraph pattern** (the guide):

```text
1. CLAIM       about a body of work, not about one paper
2. EVIDENCE    from 2–5 sources, cited
3. AGREEMENT   or CONFLICT across those sources
4. UNRESOLVED  what remains open — the wall your gap walks through
5. LINK        forward to your study
```

**Style rules.** The Phrasebank's *Referring to sources* section supplies the citation-move
phrasings. Glasman-Deal's move-structure chapters treat each review paragraph type in
depth. Zotero collections per theme keep the evidence retrievable.

**Watch out for.** The laundry list: "Smith did X. Jones did Y." One citation per paragraph
is a list item in disguise — merge or cut. (This is the failure the synthesis pattern exists
to prevent.)

**Your work.** Build the synthesis matrix (rows = your 8–10 papers, columns = 3–5 themes
your RQ cares about), then write two paragraphs off it, each citing 2+ sources in
comparison and each ending unresolved.

### 3.5 Materials & Methods — *written first*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #5 · How to Write the Materials and Methods Section (6:55)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_05_materials_methods.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Methods* (3 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/06_methods.md) · Grad Coach's methodology videos
([channel profile](02_VIDEO_COURSES/03_grad_coach.md))

**What the sources teach.** The methods section is "the paper's reproducibility contract: a
competent stranger in your field should be able to rerun the study from the text alone.
That standard — not 'sound like a scientist' — decides what stays and what goes." The
video's own focus (from its study notes): *reproducibility as a writing standard — what a
stranger needs to rerun your study.*

**The completeness audit** (the guide):

| Category | Must contain |
|:---|:---|
| Materials | what, how much, from where, which version/lot/vendor + availability statement |
| Procedure | ordered steps; randomized/blinded conditions where they exist |
| Parameters | every number with units and the reason for its value (or a sensitivity note) |
| Statistics | test, effect size measure, significance threshold, software + version |
| Ethics | IRB/ethics approval number, consent, pre-registration ID where applicable |

**Style rules.** Past tense. Voice follows venue norm — check two recent papers. Order
follows the logic of the experiment, not the chronology of your lab notebook.

**Watch out for.** The guide's length signal: "methods are the section reviewers most often
call 'under-specified' — when in doubt, add the detail; move exhaustive material to
supplementaries, not to oblivion." And the ethics edge from MIT's 21W.794: paraphrase
protocols for adapting prior methods text — cite your own past writing too.

**Your work.** Draft the five audit categories as five labeled blocks; then hand the draft
to a colleague (or run the ARES `skills/05_writing_excellence/methods_precision.md` prompt)
with one question: *rerun this study — what can't you replicate?* Fix everything they name.

### 3.6 Results — *written second*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #6 · How to Write the Results Section (8:24)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_06_results.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Results* (3 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/07_results.md)

**What the sources teach.** "Results report; they do not argue. Each unit of results is a
finding stated plainly, then the evidence for it — the number, the figure, the table — with
statistics attached. Interpretation belongs to the Discussion." The video's focus (study
notes): *reporting without editorializing — leading with the finding, then the evidence.*

**The results paragraph pattern** (the guide, with its own worked example):

```text
1. FINDING    what the data shows, in plain words
              ("Model X reduced error by 23% over the strongest baseline")
2. EVIDENCE   the exact numbers with uncertainty
              ("31.4 ± 1.2 vs. 40.8 ± 2.1 on benchmark Y, n=5 seeds")
3. POINTER    where the reader verifies it ("Fig. 3, Table 2")
4. (optional) one neutral comparison sentence — relation, never meaning
```

**Style rules.** Past tense. Structure follows the logic of the RQs, "not the chronological
order in which experiments were run." Every number travels with its uncertainty and n.

**Watch out for.** Adjectives doing a number's job ("significantly better" — by how much?),
and interpretation leaking in ("this surprising improvement shows…"). Both are the exact
failure the pattern prevents.

**Your work.** Write three finding paragraphs in the four-line pattern; sweep for bare
numbers and attach ± and n to each; ban the words *proves* and *demonstrates* from this
section entirely.

### 3.7 Discussion — *written after the results*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #7 · How to Write the Discussion Section (9:45 — the longest
video in the playlist)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_07_discussion.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Discussion* (3 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/08_discussion.md)

**What the sources teach.** The discussion is "where findings become meaning: interpret
against the RQ, compare with the literature the review established, separate what the data
proves from what it suggests, and acknowledge limits honestly. The strongest discussions
feel like a conversation with the field's best critics." The video's focus (study notes):
*the interpretation sandwich — what it means, why it matters, what it cannot claim.*

**The discussion skeleton** (the guide's six moves):

```text
1. RESTATE       the finding in one sentence — no new numbers
2. INTERPRET     what it means; the mechanism — why the result came out this way
3. CONTEXTUALIZE agreement/conflict with prior work (cite the review's anchors), and
                why the difference
4. LIMITATIONS   honest and specific — each paired with its effect on the conclusion
5. IMPLICATIONS  what changes for theory or practice
6. FUTURE WORK   one or two concrete next studies, not a wish list
```

**Style rules.** Present tense for what the finding *means*, past for what you *did*. The
Phrasebank's *Discussing findings* section supplies the hedged-comparison phrasings
("these findings are consistent with…", "this discrepancy may reflect…").

**Watch out for.** The overclaim test the guide attaches: "strike every sentence the data
cannot carry — 'proves', 'demonstrates conclusively', generalizations beyond the sample.
Hedge precisely: 'suggests within this cohort' beats vague hedging." The pairing in move 4
is the craft: a limitation *without* its consequence reads like ritual; a limitation *with*
its consequence reads like judgment.

**Your work.** Draft the six moves as six labeled blocks; then run the overclaim strike-out
(hunt every *proves*/*demonstrates conclusively* and soften or strike); check every
limitation sentence contains the phrase "which limits" or "the effect on … is therefore."

### 3.8 Conclusion — *written last, after everything else*

**Watch first:** [Ref-n-Write #8 · How to Write the Conclusion Section (4:04)](02_VIDEO_COURSES/video_notes/video_08_conclusion.md)
**Read next:** Wiley module *Conclusions* (2 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/09_conclusion.md)

**What the sources teach.** The conclusion "closes the loop: answer the RQ in one or two
sentences, restate the contribution in its most compact form, and open exactly one next
question. It is short (a paragraph in most journals; some venues merge it into the
Discussion) — which is why it is often written carelessly. A weak conclusion spends its
length re-summarizing the paper; a strong one spends it on what the reader should now
believe and do."

**The three-sentence conclusion** (the guide):

```text
1. ANSWER        "We showed that X, by [method in six words], achieving [headline result]."
2. SIGNIFICANCE  what this changes — for the sub-question, the field, or practice
3. HORIZON       the next question this opens
```

**The hard rule** (the guide's): *do not introduce new results, new citations, or new claims
in the conclusion* — if a new idea appears, it belonged in the Discussion.

**Your work.** Write exactly three sentences in the pattern; then search your conclusion for
any number or citation that appears nowhere earlier in the paper — there should be zero.

### 3.9 Figures & tables — *made during Results, audited during revision*

**Read next:** Wiley module *Figures and tables that clarify your findings* (4 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/11_figures_and_tables.md) ·
[Alley's *Craft of Scientific Writing*](03_BOOKS/03_alley_craft_of_scientific_writing.md)
for the strongest treatment of figure-text economy. (No playlist video is dedicated to
figures; they appear across videos #5–7.)

**What the sources teach.** "Figures and tables are results in their most-scanned form:
most readers' first pass is the figures, the abstract, and the conclusions — in that order.
Each display item must carry one message, be readable at print size, and be self-contained."

**The display-item checklist** (the guide):

- One message per item — if a figure makes two points, it is two figures
- Self-contained caption: what, where, n, and the key reading ("Arrow marks the divergence point")
- Legible at 100% print size — fonts ≥ 7pt at final size; axis labels with units
- Colorblind-safe palette (≈8% of male readers); never encode meaning in color alone
- Tables for exact values, figures for trends — not both for the same data
- Reference every item from the text ("Fig. 2") — an unreferenced figure is dead weight
- Rights and data availability for reused items

**Your work.** Audit every figure and table in your draft against the seven-point checklist;
kill any item that fails "one message."

### 3.10 References & citations — *managed from day one, audited before submission*

**Read next:** Wiley module *References and citations* (3 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/10_references_and_citations.md) ·
[Purdue OWL](04_FREE_GUIDES/01_purdue_owl.md) citation-style handouts ·
[IEEE Author Center](04_FREE_GUIDES/04_ieee_author_center.md) for engineering venues

**What the sources teach.** "Citations are load-bearing claims about the literature: every
one asserts that a source says what you say it says." The division of labor: "style (APA,
IEEE, Vancouver, Chicago) is venue-determined and mechanical — Zotero handles it;
correctness of *content* (right paper, right claim, right year) is scholarly judgment."

**The citation hygiene protocol** (the guide's five steps):

1. Manage in Zotero from day one — never retype a reference
2. Cite from the source: read what you cite
3. Check format against the venue template's example
4. Use the ARES `skills/09_citation_mastery/multi_format_citations.md` prompt for the venue's
   exact style
5. Audit for: orphan claims (no citation), citation-claim mismatch, duplicates, and stale
   versions of datasets/software (cite the *version you used*)

**The ethics edge** (from the guide, via MIT 21W.794): self-plagiarism needs citation too —
quote or cite your own prior text.

**Your work.** Run the five-step audit on your full draft; fix every orphan claim and every
citation-claim mismatch you find.

## Part 4 · Revision — turning a draft into a manuscript

Stanford Unit 4 ("the writing process — pre-writing, drafting, substantive revision")
separates drafting from *substantive* revision, and Duke's short lessons exist for exactly
this stage ("one lesson per day; apply to the paragraph you're currently drafting"). The
method below is that teaching, organized as passes.

### 4.1 Pass 1 · Structure — do not touch sentences

Read only the skeleton: does each section do only its own job (Results clean of
interpretation, Discussion clean of new results)? Do paragraphs have exactly one job? Then
the guide's own device: **read only the first sentence of every paragraph, in order** — that
chain should read as a coherent outline of the paper. Where the chain breaks, the structure
breaks.

### 4.2 Pass 2 · Clarity — the Part 2 drills, applied everywhere

Apply §2.1–§2.3 systematically: cut clutter (the classic first cuts list), restore verbs
from nominalizations, check parallelism in every list, reunite subjects and predicates.
Purdue's wordiness exercises and Alley's side-by-side weak/strong examples are the practice
material; the ARES `evaluators/CLARITY_ANALYZER.md` gate (target ≥80) is the automated
mirror if you use ARES. **Measure the pass**: a serious clarity pass removes ≈10% of the
words while losing no content — count before and after; the number is your proof.

### 4.3 Pass 3 · Surface — consistency and the read-aloud test

Notation consistency, abbreviations defined at first use, table/figure numbering, citation
format uniformity (Zotero handles this if you cited from it). Then read the whole paper
**aloud**: every stumble marks a sentence a reader will stumble on. (Duke's principle is
the arbiter — if the intent comes across, it was effective.)

### 4.4 The ARES gates (if you use ARES)

The library's [learning paths](08_LEARNING_PATHS/) route every revision through the four
evaluators: Clarity ≥80, and Rigor ≥75 where the paper carries empirical claims. Treat any
sub-gate as a to-do list, not a verdict.

### Your work — Part 4

- [ ] First-sentence chain reads as an outline
- [ ] Word count reduced ≈10% with no content lost (write both numbers down)
- [ ] Read-aloud pass done; every stumble fixed
- [ ] (If used) Clarity ≥80 / Rigor ≥75 gates green

## Part 5 · Submission — the last mile the courses teach

Wiley's course devotes five of its fourteen modules to things that are not manuscript
sections at all — author list, acknowledgements, the letter to the Editor-in-Chief, the
final exam — "recognizing these are decisions, not afterthoughts." This part is those
modules, plus the peer-review teaching from Stanford Unit 6 and the Nature courses.

### 5.1 Authorship & acknowledgements — decide before drafting

**Read next:** Wiley modules *The Author List* (4 lessons) + *The Importance of
Acknowledgements* (2 lessons) · [library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/13_authorship_and_ethics.md)

**What the sources teach.** "Authorship is an ethical claim about contribution, not a
courtesy or an ordering puzzle: the standard (ICMJE and most venues) is substantial
intellectual contribution plus accountability for the work." The guide's decision set:

1. **Who qualifies**: substantial contribution to conception/design/execution/analysis *and*
   drafting or critically revising *and* final approval *and* accountability — ICMJE's four
   criteria, adopted widely beyond medicine
2. **CRediT roles**: state contributions explicitly (14 standard roles) where the venue
   allows — it defuses ordering disputes
3. **Order**: field conventions differ; agree *before* drafting, in writing
4. **Who doesn't qualify but helped**: acknowledged, with permission, and funding disclosed
5. **Compliance sweep**: IRB/ethics numbers, consent, data-availability statement, conflicts
   of interest, funding sources, pre-registration IDs

ACS Author University's *Deciding Authorship Order* episode (in the
[video-courses folder](02_VIDEO_COURSES/08_acs_author_university.md)) covers the
negotiation tactfully.

### 5.2 Choose the journal

**Read next:** the [École Polytechnique course](01_COURSES/02_ecole_polytechnique_how_to_write_and_publish.md)
— its project-centered modules have you choose an appropriate journal and build a
submission-readiness checklist as course artifacts · the [Andy Stapleton channel](02_VIDEO_COURSES/02_andy_stapleton.md)
for "how the game is played" — which journal actually fits, how editors think.

**The method** (from the course's artifact-first design): shortlist three venues; read one
recent published paper per venue; compare scope fit, audience, article limits, review
model, and open-access cost; **pick on scope fit and audience first** — the venue's
readership is who your cover letter will address. École Polytechnique's readiness checklist
then cross-checks the choice against the submission requirements.

### 5.3 Write the cover letter

**Read next:** Wiley module *Letter to the Editor-in-Chief* (4 lessons) ·
[library guide](07_SECTION_GUIDES/12_cover_letter.md)

**What the sources teach.** "The cover letter is the only place you argue *fit* rather than
*findings*: why this journal, why now, and why the editors' readers specifically. Editors
triage on it — a generic letter signals a submission blasted to many venues."

**The four-paragraph cover letter** (the guide):

```text
P1 · SUBMISSION    title, article type, and the three legal sentences: original,
                   unpublished, not under consideration elsewhere
P2 · THE PAPER     problem → finding → significance, in three sentences, for the
                   journal's BROAD readership
P3 · FIT           why THIS journal — its aims & scope, the audience this serves
P4 · LOGISTICS     corresponding author, conflicts of interest, funding, suggested
                   reviewers (active authors in the area, never collaborators or
                   friends), excluded reviewers with reasons
```

**Never** (the guide's list): flattery ("your prestigious journal"), promises about impact
factors, pre-emptive defensiveness about limitations. The standard: paragraph 3 must be
unable to match any other journal.

### 5.4 The submission packet

Wiley's final exam "doubles as a pre-submission checklist — cross-check with
`evaluators/` gates." The packet the library's own
[submission step](00_GETTING_STARTED/step_12_submit_and_survive_peer_review.md) assembles:

- Manuscript in the venue's template, within every limit
- Figures as separate files at required resolution, named in order
- Cover letter (§5.3)
- Data availability statement; conflict-of-interest declarations for every author
- Author contributions (CRediT) where offered; funding statement with grant numbers
- Ethics/approval numbers in the Methods AND the submission form
- All co-authors have seen and approved the final version

### 5.5 Survive peer review

**Read next:** Stanford Unit 6 — "plagiarism, peer review, authorship criteria, predatory
journals" · [Nature's *Focus on Peer Review*](01_COURSES/12_nature_focus_on_peer_review.md)
(free; shows the cycle from the editor's chair) ·
[Nature's *Publishing a Research Paper*](01_COURSES/13_nature_publishing_a_research_paper.md)
· [Elsevier's Certified Peer Reviewer course](01_COURSES/11_elsevier_researcher_academy.md)

**What the sources teach.** A request to revise is a success, not a failure — the
[library's step 12](00_GETTING_STARTED/step_12_submit_and_survive_peer_review.md) rehearses
the reviewer-response format the courses converge on:

```text
REVIEWER:  [quote the comment verbatim]
RESPONSE:  agree or contest, with evidence; then say exactly what changed
           and where (page, line); never argue tone
```

Reread every comment twice before answering any; respond point-by-point; resubmit with a
summary letter. On rejection: the paper routes to venue #2 with a one-day turnaround,
changed only by whatever the reviews taught you.

### Your work — Part 5

- [ ] Authorship decided and agreed in writing (CRediT roles where offered)
- [ ] Three-venue comparison done, one chosen, reason statable in a sentence
- [ ] Cover letter drafted with P3 specific to the chosen journal
- [ ] Packet checklist confirmed against the venue's own submission page
- [ ] One reviewer response rehearsed in the quote → agree/contest → what-changed format
