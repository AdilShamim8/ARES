# Step 4 of 12 — Find your research question

| | |
|:---|:---|
| **Phase** | 1 · Foundations (Week 1) |
| **Time** | ≈ 90 minutes |
| **Deliverable** | ONE research question that passes four tests, plus a three-sentence gap statement |
| **Before you start** | [Step 3](step_03_read_papers_like_a_researcher.md) complete — the limitations you collected there are the raw material today |

> **The one idea of this step:** the research question is the load-bearing beam of a paper.
> Every other section is an answer, an evidence, or a defense of it. A weak RQ makes a weak
> paper *regardless of how well it is written.*

## Why this step matters

This is the highest-leverage 90 minutes of the entire roadmap. A question that is focused,
answerable, falsifiable, and sized to one paper will carry you to submission; a vague theme
("I want to study climate effects on education") will collapse in the Introduction and no
amount of prose skill will save it. Senior researchers spend disproportionate time here
precisely because everything downstream is mechanical once the beam is set.

## What you will do — the topic-to-RQ ladder

**Rung 1 · Topic → question.** Add *what / how / why / when / to what extent* and a
measurable object.

```text
Topic:     "spaced repetition and studying"
Question:  "How does spaced repetition affect exam performance?"
```

**Rung 2 · Question → focused question.** Bind it to a system, a population, a scale, a
comparison. The classic scaffolding: *Does [specific intervention] improve [specific outcome]
in [specific population] compared to [specific comparison]?*

```text
Focused:   "Does weekly spaced-repetition quizzing improve final-exam performance
            in first-year biology students compared to massed restudy?"
```

**Rung 3 · Focused question → hypothesis (empirical work).** Commit to a direction you can
refute:

```text
Hypothesis: Students receiving weekly spaced-repetition quizzes will score higher on the
            final exam than students restudying the same material, with the gap persisting
            on a 3-month retention test.
```

## Your work

### Work 4.1 — Draft three candidate RQs (30 min)

From your Step 3 notes (especially the LIMITATION rows — five limitations side by side usually
*are* a gap), draft three different focused questions using the scaffolding above. Do not
self-censor yet; quantity first.

### Work 4.2 — Apply the four tests (30 min)

Score each candidate against:

| Test | Question it asks | Fail looks like |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Focused** | One question or a theme? | "effects of technology on learning" |
| **Answerable** | Do data/method exist or are collectable by you? | Needs a national registry you can't access |
| **Falsifiable** | Can a result refute it? | " quizzes are good" — no refutable outcome |
| **Sized** | One paper's worth? (not a program of research) | Needs 3 studies and 2 years |

Pick the winner — usually the one scoring 4/4 that uses data you can actually get. Write the
other two down anyway: they are your future papers (Step 12 returns to them).

### Work 4.3 — Write the gap statement (30 min)

The gap statement is the three-sentence core of your future Introduction, following the classic
CARS structure (Create A Research Space — Swales' model; see
[`../03_BOOKS/08_swales_feak_academic_writing_graduate_students.md`](../03_BOOKS/08_swales_feak_academic_writing_graduate_students.md)):

```text
TERRITORY:   [Topic] matters because [concrete stake, 1–2 sentences with citations].
NICHE:       However, it remains unknown whether [specific gap — drawn from your
             Step-3 limitation rows].
OCCUPATION:  This study addresses that gap by [method in six words] on [population/data],
             measuring [outcome].
```

**Practice example (hypothetical):**

> *Retrieval practice improves retention across many classroom settings (cite your two pillar
> papers). However, it remains unknown whether weekly spaced-retrieval quizzes improve
> retention measured months after course end, rather than at the final exam. This study
> addresses that gap with a two-group classroom experiment (N = 64) in first-year biology,
> measuring both final-exam performance and a 3-month retention test.*

One paragraph, three moves, zero filler — that is the density reviewers expect.

## Exit test

- [ ] One RQ passes all four tests, written in the focused-question scaffolding
- [ ] A hypothesis exists for it (empirical work) with a refutable direction
- [ ] The gap statement's NICHE sentence traces to specific limitations from your Step-3 notes
- [ ] Two spare RQs parked for the future
