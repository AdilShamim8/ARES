# Step 3 of 12 — Read papers like a researcher

| | |
|:---|:---|
| **Phase** | 1 · Foundations (Week 1) |
| **Time** | ≈ 90 minutes today, then 30–60 min/day as a habit |
| **Deliverable** | Five completed reading notes — your first evidence pile |
| **Before you start** | [Step 2](step_02_know_the_paper_anatomy.md) complete |

> **The one idea of this step:** beginners read linearly, cover to cover, and remember
> nothing. Researchers read in passes, extract into notes, and only ever *search* their notes
> afterward.

## Why this step matters

Your literature review (Step 5) and your Introduction (Step 8) are built entirely out of what
you capture now. Notes made during reading are the difference between a review that
*synthesizes* ("three of five studies agree, two conflict, none tested X") and one that
*summarizes* ("Smith did A. Jones did B. Lee did C…") — the first gets published; the second
gets rejected as "a list, not a review."

## What you will do — the three passes

**Pass 1 · Triage (5 minutes per paper).** Read title, abstract, all section headings, and the
final paragraph of the Introduction (where the RQ lives). Decide: *does this paper matter to
me?* Three answers: yes / maybe / no. "No" papers are closed forever — this is a skill, not a
failure.

**Pass 2 · Map (15 minutes).** For "yes" papers: read the figures and tables, the first
paragraph of the Discussion, and the Methods only where the design is unclear. Answer: *what
did they do, and what did they find?*

**Pass 3 · Deep read (45+ minutes, only when needed).** Only for papers you will cite as
pillars — your closest competitors, your methodological source. Read fully, including the
supplementary material if it carries the evidence.

## Your work

### Work 3.1 — Fill five reading notes (≈75 min)

Use this exact template — one note per paper, stored in Zotero's note field for that item
(click the paper → *Add note*) or in a plain document:

```text
PAPER:            [authors, year, title]
MY QUESTION IT FEEDS:   [one line — how this paper relates to my RQ]
THEIR RQ:          [their research question, one sentence]
DESIGN:            [method, sample/data, duration — 2 lines]
KEY FINDING:       [the headline result, WITH the number]
LIMITATION:        [what the authors admit OR what I see as the gap]
QUOTABLE PHRASE:   [one sentence of theirs worth reusing the PATTERN of (never the words)]
MY VERDICT:        pillar / supporting / background / discard
```

The **LIMITATION** row is gold: five limitations from five papers, laid side by side, usually
*are* your research gap. That is how Step 4 will find your RQ — almost mechanically.

### Work 3.2 — Sort the pile (10 min)

Tag each of the five notes with its verdict: **pillar** (must engage deeply), **supporting**
(cite in passing), **background** (establishes context), **discard**. Pillars get a Pass 3
sometime this week; the others do not.

## Practice example — one filled note (hypothetical study shown for craft only)

```text
PAPER:            Okoye & Martins, 2025, "Spaced retrieval practice in large lectures"
MY QUESTION IT FEEDS: direct competitor — same intervention, different population
THEIR RQ:          Does weekly spaced retrieval improve exam scores in second-year pharmacy?
DESIGN:            Two-group quasi-experiment, N=180, one semester, ANCOVA on midterm scores
KEY FINDING:       +6.2 percentage points on the final (95% CI 2.1–10.3), adjusted
LIMITATION:        Single institution; no 3-month retention test; volunteer lecturers
QUOTABLE PHRASE:   "…retention was measured at course end rather than across a delay interval"
MY VERDICT:        pillar
```

(Practice example — this paper is invented to show the craft of note-taking, not to report a
real study. Your five notes will be about *real* papers in *your* field.)

## Exit test

- [ ] Five notes complete, each with DESIGN, KEY FINDING (with a number), and LIMITATION
- [ ] Every note carries a MY-QUESTION line tying it to your (upcoming) RQ
- [ ] Each paper tagged pillar / supporting / background / discard
